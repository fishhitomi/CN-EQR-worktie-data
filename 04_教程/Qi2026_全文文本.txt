 Do Engagement Quality Reviewers’
  Workplace Ties with Engagement
  Partners Influence Audit Quality?

                                           ABSTRACT

   The engagement quality review is a key component of an audit firm’s quality
   control system. This study leverages unique data on individual engagement
   quality reviewers (EQRs) to examine how previous shared working experi-
   ence between EQRs and engagement partners affects audit quality. While
   prior research suggests that within-firm network ties between predecessor and
   successor partners facilitate knowledge transfer, we find that prior shared
   working experience between EQRs and engagement partners is associated
   with lower audit quality. Mechanism tests indicate that such experience is asso-
   ciated with a higher likelihood of regulatory enforcement actions related to
   deficiencies in audit procedures, insufficient evidence, and a lack of profes-
   sional skepticism. We also find that such experience corresponds with higher
   materiality thresholds, suggesting reduced scrutiny during audit planning

1. Introduction
Engagement quality reviews (hereafter EQ review) play an integral role
in an accounting firm’s system of quality management. Despite their
important role in promoting audit quality, empirical evidence on their
effectiveness remains scarce due to the limited availability of public infor-
mation.1 As highlighted by Lennox et al. [2026], key participants in the
audit process, such as engagement quality reviewers (EQRs), remain under-
examined in the academic literature—particularly with respect to whether
relationships between EQRs and members of audit engagement teams
compromise the objectivity of the EQ review. Auditing standards require
the EQR to be independent of the audit engagement team and to provide
an objective evaluation of the team’s key judgments and conclusions in
forming the audit opinion (Tucker and Matsumura [1997], Lennox et al.
[2020]).2 However, the standards also highlight the importance of commu-
nication and coordination between the EQR and the engagement partner

   Although recent studies highlight the informational effects of engage-
ment partners’ network connections (Huang et al. [2021], Pittman et al.
[2022]), there is a paucity of research examining network connections
with EQRs. The EQR’s unique function differs from that of engagement
partners in important ways. First, unlike the interdependent relation-
ship between two engagement partners, the role of the EQR is designed to
enable objective views and to exercise, when necessary, a constraining influ-
ence on questionable judgments (Jamal et al. [1995], Rittenberg [2008]).
The EQR serves as a quality control designed to mitigate within-firm agency
problems, such as free-riding (Balachandran and Ramakrishnan [1987],
Bedard et al. [2008]). Second, these roles are evaluated using different
performance criteria due to their distinct responsibilities within the audit
firm (Lennox et al. [2020]). Engagement partners are assessed on both
audit quality and engagement profitability, whereas EQRs are primarily
evaluated based on audit quality. Third, the communication requirements
differ. While discussion between the two engagement partners and within
the engagement team is mandatory, communication between the EQR and
the engagement team is permitted but must not compromise the EQR’s
objectivity (PCAOB [2008a], CICPA [2021]). Given the distinct roles
and incentive structures of EQRs and engagement partners, prior shared
working experience may have different implications for audit quality.
   On the one hand, workplace ties are often presumed to enhance audit
quality by fostering communication and coordination during the review
process. Recent studies on engagement partners’ network connections
generally find that shared working experience leads to desirable audit
outcomes, likely through facilitating information exchange (Huang et al.
[2021], Pittman et al. [2022]). Benefits include reduced misunderstand-
ings, increased trust, and enhanced knowledge sharing (Sias and Cahill
[1998], Reagans et al. [2005], Vera-Munoz et al. [2006], Bianchi [2018],
Pittman et al. [2022]). Such knowledge sharing and collaboration can con-
tribute to a more effective and efficient EQ review, ultimately improving
audit quality.
   On the other hand, given the disparate roles and incentive schemes as-
sumed by EQRs and engagement partners (Lennox et al. [2020]), greater
familiarity could weaken EQR independence, thereby undermining the ef-
fectiveness of the EQ review and increasing the possibility of biased or less
effective audit judgments and outcomes. Prior research on social ties be-
tween monitors and those being monitored highlights that close relation-
ships and unwarranted trust can impair audit quality (Menon and Williams
[2004], Lennox [2005], Bruynseels and Cardinaels [2014], Guan et al.

[2016], He et al. [2017]). Moreover, social exchange theory suggests that
EQRs’ prior collaboration with engagement partners may cultivate trust,
fostering homophily and increasing the risk of groupthink (Cropanzano
and Mitchell [2005]). This, in turn, may elevate the likelihood of confirma-
tion bias and overreliance on the engagement partner’s judgments (Moore
et al. [2006], Downar et al. [2021], Ertug et al. [2022]). Given these com-
peting arguments, the influence of EQRs’ and engagement partners’ prior
shared working experience on audit quality remains an open empirical
question.
   We perform our examination using EQR data from China. The Chinese
setting offers three significant advantages. First, on February 28, 2020,
the Shanghai Stock Exchange and the Shenzhen Stock Exchange of
China jointly revised the template for listed companies to announce the
appointment of accounting firms and mandated the disclosure of detailed
information about EQRs. This unique institutional feature enables us to
identify the EQR for each audit engagement and obtain detailed informa-
tion about their professional profile. Second, leveraging the long history
of engagement partner data from China, we can determine the closeness
or shared working experience between the EQR and the engagement
partners by tracking their joint signing history in audit reports or capital
market projects. Third, two complementary regulatory disclosures in China
offer unique insights into audit practices: the types of violations identified
in public audit enforcement actions, and audit materiality thresholds
identified from modified audit opinions. Since March 2021, the China
Securities Regulatory Commission (CSRC) has required auditors to dis-
close overall quantitative materiality of consolidated financial statements
in special explanations of modified audit opinions, including the bench-
mark, applied percentage, calculated amount, and rationale for selection.
These disclosures provide rare visibility into audit planning and execution,
enabling a deeper understanding of the mechanisms through which EQRs’
prior shared working experience with engagement partners may influence
audit judgments and performance.
   Our sample comprises 16,241 audit engagements in China between 2020
and 2023.3 In China, audit reports are signed by two auditors. In our sam-
ple, approximately 98% of these auditors are partners, and we refer to
them as such following the convention in prior research (Lennox et al.
[2020]). Because the identities of other team members (e.g., audit man-
agers or audit staff) are not publicly disclosed, our measure is limited to
shared working experience as more experienced auditors. In our sample,
approximately 9.2% of EQRs oversee engagement partners with whom they
have previously collaborated on engagements other than the focal client,

   We begin by examining whether connected EQRs are associated with
various audit quality proxies. Specifically, we use (1) the absolute value
of discretionary accruals, (2) modified audit opinions, and (3) misstate-
ments revealed through subsequent financial statement restatements and
regulatory sanctions. While we acknowledge the limitations associated
with each of these proxies, we recognize that evidence triangulated across
multiple complementary proxies can be more compelling (DeFond and
Zhang [2014]). We find that audit engagements with a connected EQR are
associated with a 1.1% decrease in the likelihood of receiving a modified
audit opinion, a 1.5% increase in the probability of misstatement revealed
through subsequent financial restatements or regulatory sanctions, and
exhibit higher abnormal accruals. Collectively, these results suggest lower
audit quality outcomes when the EQR and engagement partners have prior
shared working experience.
   Our results are consistent when using alternative proxies for audit
quality, including discretionary accruals estimated using alternative mod-
els, audit reporting aggressiveness, going-concern modifications, and
egregious financial misstatements identified through audit enforcement
actions. Additionally, we find consistent results using an alternative mea-
sure of within-firm shared working experience grounded in social network
analysis. We address selection and endogeneity concerns using a variety of
additional tests, including a Heckman selection model, change analyses,
as well as falsification and placebo tests. The collective results provide
corroborative support for our primary findings.
   To provide greater insight into the mechanisms through which prior
shared working experience affects audit quality, we conduct two additional
analyses using unique data sources from the Chinese audit setting: the types
of violations identified in public audit enforcement actions and audit mate-
riality levels reported in modified audit opinions. Our results indicate that
the presence of a connected EQR increases the likelihood of audit enforce-
ment related to deficiencies in audit procedures, insufficient evidence, and
a lack of professional skepticism. Furthermore, it is associated with higher
materiality thresholds, suggesting reduced scrutiny in audit planning and
execution.
   We also perform cross-sectional analyses to examine whether the impact
of connected EQRs on audit quality varies with the nature of the previous
shared working experience, EQRs’ incentives, or EQRs’ reputational con-
cerns. The results suggest that the association between connected EQRs
and lower audit quality primarily arises when previous shared working re-
lationships did not produce adverse outcomes, when EQRs are not audit
industry leaders, and when they face lower reputational risk. These results

highlight the specific circumstances under which workplace ties can lead
to less effective EQ reviews.
   Finally, we examine the influence of EQRs’ prior shared working ex-
perience with engagement partners on EQR assignments. Our evidence
suggests that EQR candidates with such experience are nearly 1.56 times as
likely to be selected as those without such experience. In terms of marginal
effects, the increase in selection probability associated with prior shared
working experience is roughly five times as large as that associated with in-
dustry expertise, indicating that previous shared working experience with
either engagement partner appears to be even more influential. Addition-
ally, candidates with longer professional tenure, higher authority, closer
geographic proximity, and demographic similarity to the engagement part-
ners are more likely to be appointed as the EQR, while compliance with
professional standards and workload considerations also play an important
role in assignment decisions.
   These findings provide several important contributions. First, while prior
research finds that co-working experience between engagement partners
facilitates information exchange, thus leading to desirable audit outcomes
(Huang et al. [2021], Pittman et al. [2022]), our findings contribute to the
existing research on the role of within-firm networks in accounting and fi-
nance by highlighting the circumstances when EQRs’ prior shared working
experience with engagement partners adversely influences audit quality.4
   Second, our study contributes to the long-standing research on internal
monitoring of audit quality. EQ reviews have been required for public
company audits in many jurisdictions, including the United States and
China (AICPA [2004], IAASB [2005], CICPA [2006]). However, there
is limited empirical evidence on the effectiveness of EQ reviews. Our
study directly responds to calls from Emby and Favere-Marchesi [2010]
and Lennox et al. [2026] for more research on EQRs to examine their
influence on audit outcomes and to complement existing experimental
and survey-based evidence (Favere-Marchesi and Emby [2005], Emby and
Favere-Marchesi [2010], Chan et al. [2024]).
   In addition, our study answers the call for research on the partner assign-
ment process (Lennox and Wu [2018]). While prior research examines
engagement partner assignments and the partner–client matching process
(Firth et al. [2012], Dodgson et al. [2020], Francis et al. [2022], Pittman
et al. [2022], Wu et al. [2024]), we provide novel evidence on EQR as-
signments and the consequences of assigning EQRs who have prior shared
working experience with engagement partners. Our results suggest that

   Our study also has implications for both practitioners and regulators.
For practitioners, our findings have direct relevance for EQR assignments.
Audit firm leadership may consider the importance of enhancing EQR
objectivity by assigning EQRs who are less connected to the engagement
partner or have experienced negative outcomes together on previous
shared engagements, as well as EQRs who possess greater expertise or
stronger reputational incentives. For regulators, our study provides timely
insights into EQR independence. Our evidence directly addresses regu-
latory concerns on the boundaries of communication and consultation
between the EQR and the engagement team—issues debated extensively
during the PCAOB’s deliberations on Auditing Standard No. 7 (KPMG
[2008], PCAOB [2008b, 2008c]). Our results highlight circumstances
when EQR independence is more likely to be impaired. Given the trend
in quality control deficiencies related to EQ reviews [PCAOB 2023], our
results can also help regulators tailor inspections to audit engagements
that pose the greatest risk of ineffective EQ review.


2. Institutional Background and Literature Review
2.1 international background and regulation of eq review
  EQ reviews have long been required for public companies under au-
diting standards (PCAOB [2003], AICPA [2004], IAASB [2005], CICPA
[2006]). This quality control mechanism at the engagement level is widely
acknowledged by regulatory bodies, investors, and audit professionals as
a pivotal mechanism in ensuring high-quality audits (Christensen et al.
[2016]). The Chinese auditing standards on EQ review are largely conver-
gent with both US and International Standards on Auditing, reflecting a
high degree of alignment in objectives, implementation, and requirements
regarding the assignment and qualifications of EQRs. This convergence
supports the broader international relevance of our study’s findings.
Both China and the United States require EQRs who are not part of the
8      y. qi, t. a. seidel, j. h. zhang, and j. zhang

audit engagement team to conduct the review from the perspective of an
independent outsider (PCAOB [2009], CICPA [2020]). Moreover, both
sets of standards provide guidance on how EQRs should communicate
with the engagement team in a manner that does not compromise their
independence.
   In the United States, the SEC Practice Section of the American In-
stitute of Certified Public Accountants (henceforth SECPS) initiated the
requirement for its member accounting firms to undertake EQ reviews
for public company audits in 1977.5 By 1986, SECPS further delineated
brief stipulations regarding EQR qualifications, the scope of duties, and
documentation requirements. In 1987, an observation by the Treadway
Commission revealed a marked disparity in the EQ review standards and
practices among accounting firms, leading to a recommendation for the
SECPS to revisit and enhance the pertinent benchmarks for EQ review. The
SEC later took action against EQRs who failed to meet their professional
obligations.6
   In 2002, the US Congress enacted the Sarbanes-Oxley Act, compelling
the PCAOB to develop explicit auditing standards for EQ review. Con-
currently, SECPS introduced two distinct sections within its stipulations
for member firms (1000.08(f) and 1000.39) that articulated basic require-
ments pertinent to EQ reviews. In 2004, the Standing Advisory Group
(SAG) affiliated with the PCAOB provided a discussion draft related to the
EQR standard. In 2009, based on the public feedback amassed by the SAG,
the PCAOB issued Auditing Standard No. 7 (codified as AS No. 1220) to re-
place its interim requirement for EQ review based on guidelines instituted
by the SECPS in 2002. In 2020, the International Auditing and Assurance
Standards Board (IAASB) revised the “International Standard on Quality
Management 1, Quality Management for Firms That Perform Audits or Reviews of
Financial Statements, or Other Assurance or Related Services Engagements” (ISQM
1) alongside the “International Standard on Auditing 220, Quality Control for an
Audit of Financial Statements” (ISA 220). Concurrently, it introduced a new
standard, the “International Standard on Quality Management 2, Engagement
Quality Reviews” (ISQM 2) (IAASB [2020]). This standalone standard high-
lights the IAASB’s view of the significance of EQ reviews to audit quality.

2.2 chinese background and regulation of eq review
   The Chinese standards on EQ review are similar to those in the United
States. In February 2006, the Chinese Institute of Certified Public Accoun-
tants (CICPA) promulgated the “Chinese CPA Auditing Standard No. 1121,
Quality Control for an Audit of Historical Financial Statements” and the “Chi-
nese Accounting Firm Quality Control Standard No. 5101, Engagement Quality
Control.” Standard No. 5101 specifies that EQ reviews apply to audits of
listed entities and other engagements determined by accounting firms. In
November 2020, leveraging the most recent revisions to international qual-
ity management standards (ISQM 1 and ISQM 2), the CICPA revised stan-
dards No. 1121 and No. 5101, and promulgated the “Chinese Accounting Firm
Quality Management Standard No. 5102, Engagement Quality Review.” As a re-
sult, the regulatory framework for EQ review in China is highly comparable
to that in jurisdictions applying the IAASB’s international standards (ISQM
2), as well as to the US standards issued by the PCAOB (AS No. 1220).
   Under China’s Auditing Standards No. 5101 and 5102, EQRs are
required to perform a broad set of responsibilities covering the audit
planning, execution, and reporting stages. As summarized in appendix A,
these responsibilities can be broadly characterized as encompassing both
evaluative and advisory functions.
   In their evaluative role, EQRs are tasked with assessing whether signifi-
cant judgments made by the engagement team are appropriate, well sup-
ported, and sufficiently documented. This includes reviewing information
arising from engagement team communications and firm-level monitoring
processes, with particular attention to identified deficiencies and matters
involving heightened judgment or complexity. EQRs also examine working
papers related to significant matters, including the overall audit strategy,
specific audit plans, the execution of audit procedures, and the final con-
clusions, to evaluate whether professional skepticism has been appropri-
ately exercised and whether conclusions are consistent with the underlying
evidence.
   EQRs also interact with the engagement partner and audit team in an
advisory and consultative role. These interactions facilitate the exchange of
information on significant matters and areas involving complex or subjec-
tive judgments, allowing EQRs to obtain a comprehensive understanding of
the engagement and to assess the appropriateness of the team’s judgments
and related responses. Through ongoing discussions, EQRs are involved in
the consideration of difficult or controversial matters, including situations
involving differences of opinion, to ensure that such issues are appropri-
ately resolved and documented. Information obtained through these inter-
actions also informs EQRs’ assessment of compliance with independence
requirements and the adequacy of the engagement partner’s involvement,
particularly in areas requiring heightened professional judgment. Finally,
EQRs document their review work and review the final engagement deliv-
erables before the audit report is issued.

2.3 prior research related to eq review
   Despite regulators’ and standard setters’ vocal emphasis on EQ reviews,
there is a paucity of empirical evidence evaluating their efficacy, given
limited disclosure and available public information. As a result, prior
research has primarily relied on experimental or survey research methods.
Extant research primarily discusses EQ review practices (Epps and Messier
[2007]), examines EQ review effectiveness (Messier et al. [2010], Stefaniak
et al. [2017]), and investigates the impact of specific EQR attributes on
review outcomes (Lennox et al. [2020], Chan et al. [2024]). Epps and
Messier [2007] analyze six public accounting firms’ internal guidance for
EQ reviews and find a moderate level of consistency across firms, with
some differences in the guidance for EQR assignment. With regard to
EQ review effectiveness, experimental research and surveys show that EQ
reviews not only enhance the independence and prudence of engagement
partners (Tan [1995], Tucker and Matsumura [1997], Ayers and Kaplan
[2003]) but also induce engagement partners to plan higher levels of
audit sampling (Matsumura and Tucker [1995]). Moreover, EQRs create
new solutions to resolve disagreements by negotiating with engagement
partners (Emby and Favere-Marchesi [2010]) and increase audit hours to
address risks associated with CEO turnover (Kim et al. [2024]). Regarding
EQR attributes, prior research finds that EQR tenure and pressure from
engagement partners in executive positions of accounting firms both
influence EQR independence (Favere-Marchesi and Emby [2005], Chan
et al. [2024]). Additionally, EQR ownership in an accounting firm induces
greater monitoring of audit quality (Lennox et al. [2020]).
   However, due to limited disclosure, little is known about the EQR assign-
ment process and the influence of EQR and engagement partner pairings
on audit outcomes. Furthermore, existing studies on individual EQRs pri-
marily focus on specific traits in the context of the review role (Ayers and
Kaplan [2003], Favere-Marchesi and Emby [2005]), without consideration
for the proximity and connectedness of EQRs and engagement partners.

3. Hypothesis Development
  Auditing standards encourage communication and coordination be-
tween the EQR and the engagement team, while adding a strong caution
to ensure the EQR’s objectivity (PCAOB 02008a], [2009]). Previous shared
working experience between an EQR and the engagement partners could
plausibly affect audit quality in two opposing ways.
3.1 communication efficiency hypothesis
  The Communication Efficiency Hypothesis (H1-a) posits that prior col-
laboration could improve audit quality by facilitating coordination and
knowledge transfer. Recent research indicates that engagement partners’
prior shared working experience can enhance the efficiency and quality
of the audit through knowledge sharing and information transfer (Huang
  engagement quality reviewers’ workplace ties and audit quality             11

et al. [2021], Pittman et al. [2022]). Although this research focuses on en-
gagement partners with complementary incentives and roles, prior collab-
oration between an EQR and engagement partner may lead to improved
communication and better coordination.
   Research examining shared working experience highlights certain
benefits such as reduced misunderstandings, less mistrust, and greater
willingness to share information (Reagans et al. [2005], Bianchi [2018],
Pittman et al. [2022]). Causholli et al. [2021] find that auditors seek
knowledge from friends within the same audit office, from immediate
coworkers, and from those with whom they have previously worked on
an audit engagement. Moreover, the social network literature portrays co-
working experiences as employment-based social connections and argues
that stronger ties facilitate the transfer of complex knowledge (Hansen
[1999]). Workplace friendships enable coworkers to discuss sensitive issues
that might otherwise go unshared (Sias and Cahill [1998]). Vera-Munoz
et al. [2006] posit that working closely with supervisors can increase su-
pervised auditors’ knowledge sharing and willingness to seek advice or
disclose unfavorable findings. Similarly, Asare and McDaniel [1996] show
that reviewers with prior interactions with the preparer are more focused
and better at detecting conclusion errors.
   Thus, greater familiarity between an EQR and engagement partners
through prior collaboration may facilitate communication, leading to more
consultations on areas of audit judgment and greater EQR involvement,
which in turn could improve audit quality. Furthermore, shared working ex-
perience can allow the EQR to learn about the engagement partners’ qual-
ity over time and use this knowledge to monitor them more effectively or
efficiently. These arguments form the basis for the following hypothesis:

  H1-a: The Communication Efficiency Hypothesis: Previous shared working
        experience between the EQR and the engagement team is positively associ-
        ated with audit quality.

3.2 independence hypothesis
  In contrast, the Independence Hypothesis (H1-b) posits that prior
shared working relationships may compromise EQR independence and
objectivity, potentially reducing audit quality. Given the oversight role that
EQRs play in the audit process, greater familiarity with the engagement
partner(s) being reviewed through previous shared working experiences
could reduce professional skepticism and oversight effectiveness. One of
the basic tenets of social exchange theory is that “relationships evolve
into trusting, loyal, and mutual commitments” (Cropanzano and Mitchell
[2005], p. 875). As auditors work together, they develop mutual trust
through shared experiences and interactions. Greater trust between an
EQR and a familiar engagement partner can create expectations of reci-
procity, wherein actions by one party elicit responses in kind (Gergen
[1969], Tanis and Postmes [2005]). This proclivity can lead to leniency in

EQR monitoring, particularly if the engagement partner might later serve
as a reviewer on the EQR’s own engagements. Consequently, the EQR may
ask fewer probing questions and be less likely to challenge the engagement
team’s judgments and conclusions.
   The partnership structure of audit firms aligns partner incentives at the
firm level, as partners share in overall profits and losses. However, this
does not fully eliminate internal agency problems, such as shirking or
overreliance on colleagues. In audit firms, auditors provide services as part
of engagement teams, complicating the assessment of individual contribu-
tions and exacerbating information asymmetry (Huddart and Liang [2003,
2005], Liu and Simunic [2005]). Partners serve as both owners and agents
(Kandel and Lazear [1992], Coram and Robinson [2017]). As owners,
audit partners have an interest in maximizing partnership profits, reducing
litigation or reputation costs, and properly incentivizing fellow partners
and professional staff. As agents, partners have incentives to minimize per-
sonal effort and to maximize compensation without necessarily considering
potential adverse effects on other partners. Prior research shows that the
risk of auditor free-riding can be mitigated by rewarding high-performing
partners (Burrows and Black [1998], Knechel et al. [2013], Coram and
Robinson [2017]) and through internal monitoring systems and peer pres-
sure (Balachandran and Ramakrishnan [1987], Kandel and Lazear [1992],
Stefaniak et al. [2017]). The EQR functions as a quality control to mitigate
such within-firm agency problems (Bedard et al. [2008]). However, prior
shared working experience may, in some cases, undermine this role by
fostering excessive trust or leniency.
   There is a notable commonality between engagement partner–client
and EQR–engagement partner relationships, as both involve monitoring.
Prior research highlights how auditors’ close relationships with clients
can impair audit quality (Menon and Williams [2004], Lennox [2005],
Guan et al. [2016], He et al. [2017], Daoust and Malsch [2020]), and
experimental evidence suggests that more trusting auditors tend to be less
skeptical (Rose [2007]), especially in circumstances that warrant increased
skepticism (Hobson et al. [2020]). However, unlike the engagement
partner–client relationship, which involves external oversight constrained
by market and regulatory forces, the EQR–engagement partner relation-
ship occurs within the same audit firm and is embedded in partnership
and internal social structures. This intra-firm setting introduces distinct
dynamics of collegiality, hierarchy, and interdependence, making EQR
independence both institutionally and socially more complex. Research on
monitors’ social ties with those they monitor is also relevant. For example,
Bruynseels and Cardinaels [2014] find that companies with more social
ties between audit committee members and CEOs engage in greater earn-
ings management. As such, relationships between EQRs and engagement
partners developed through prior collaboration on audit engagements
may weaken EQRs’ professional skepticism and willingness to challenge
the engagement partner’s judgments.
   engagement quality reviewers’ workplace ties and audit quality                         13

   In addition to trust and reciprocity, sharing similar auditing experiences
through collaboration may lead partners to develop homophily, thereby in-
creasing their susceptibility to groupthink and conformity. Through shared
auditing experiences, they are more likely to develop similar viewpoints re-
garding audit judgments, which can increase the risk that the EQR will
exhibit confirmation bias (Downar et al. [2021]) or overreliance on the
engagement partner’s judgments (Ertug et al. [2022]). Further, behavioral
convergence within social networks can stifle innovation and competition
(Bianchi et al. [2023]). In the context of EQ reviews, this may result in less
rigorous challenges of risk assessments, materiality judgments, and audit
procedure decisions than would occur with an EQR possessing different
perspectives or varied experiences. Based on this reasoning, we propose
the following alternative hypothesis:
   H1-b: The Independence Hypothesis: Previous shared working experience be-
         tween the EQR and the engagement team is negatively associated with au-
         dit quality.

4.2 empirical model
   To examine the influence of prior shared working experience between
the EQR and the engagement partners on audit quality, we estimate the
following OLS regression model with standard errors clustered by each
unique client firm and controlling for industry-, year-, and audit firm-fixed
effects:
       ABSDA/MAO/FINIRREG = α + βWORKTIE + γ CONTROLS
                                            +FIXED_EFFECTS + ε.                            (1)
   We use three measures of audit quality as dependent variables in equa-
tion (1). The first, ABSDA, is the absolute value of discretionary accruals es-
timated following Jones [1991]. The second, MAO, is an indicator variable
equal to one for fiscal years in which a client firm receives (1) an unqual-
ified audit opinion with explanatory notes, (2) a qualified audit opinion,
(3) a disclaimer, or (4) an adverse audit opinion, and zero otherwise. As
suggested by Lennox et al. [2026], MAOs align closely with DeAngelo’s
[1981] definition of auditor independence. Issuing a modified opinion
demonstrates the auditor’s ability to detect a breach and willingness to
insist on appropriate disclosures or qualifications when circumstances
warrant (DeFond et al. [1999]). Prior research, therefore, interprets a
modified audit opinion as indicative of higher audit quality and greater
auditor independence (e.g., Guan et al. [2016], Gunn et al. [2023]).
   The third, FINIRREG, is an indicator variable that equals one if the
client’s financial statements are subsequently restated due to accounting
errors or sanctioned by the CSRC for financial misrepresentation, and zero
otherwise, following DeFond et al. [2024]. Prior literature provides strong
validation for the use of misstatements revealed through subsequent re-
statements as an audit quality proxy (Christensen et al. [2016], Aobdia
[2019], Rajgopal et al. [2021], Lennox et al. [2026]). While each of these
proxies has certain limitations, evidence triangulated across multiple com-
plementary proxies can provide credible support for conclusions (DeFond
and Zhang [2014]).

equal to one if the EQR has previously served as a signing auditor on the
same engagement team as at least one of the two engagement partners on
the same listed company audit or capital market project (including annual
audits, IPOs, convertible bonds, seasoned equity offerings, asset restructur-
ings, private placements, etc.)—excluding the current focal client—zero
otherwise.9 Equation (1) includes industry, year, and audit firm-fixed

5. Empirical Results
5.1 main results
   Table 3 presents the results of the primary tests of our alternative
hypotheses. In column 1, the coefficient on WORKTIE is positive and
significant at the 5% level (coef. = 0.008; t-stat. = 2.144), indicating that
engagements reviewed by a connected EQR are associated with higher
abnormal accruals, consistent with lower audit quality. In column 2, the
coefficient is negative and significant at the 5% level (coef. = −0.011; t-stat.
= −2.184), suggesting that the probability of receiving a modified audit
opinion is 1.1% points lower when a connected EQR is present. In column
3, the coefficient is positive and significant at the 10% level (coef. = 0.015;
t-stat. = 1.683), indicating that engagements reviewed by a connected EQR
are 1.5% points more likely to be subsequently restated or sanctioned.
Overall, these results support the Independence Hypothesis (H1-b) and
suggest that, on average, EQRs’ prior shared working experience with
engagement partners undermines the effectiveness of the EQ review,
resulting in lower-quality audit outcomes. These findings are consistent
with arguments that prior shared working experiences between EQRs and
engagement partners can impair EQR objectivity and independence.

5.3 client selection and endogeneity concerns
  A major empirical challenge in assessing the effect of EQRs on audit out-
comes is that the assignment of EQRs to client engagements may not be
random. Specifically, more complex or higher-risk engagements may lead
audit firms to pair engagement partners with EQRs who have collaborated
previously. We recognize that both observable and unobservable factors
may be correlated with both audit quality and with the selection or appoint-
ment of connected EQRs. To address these concerns, we employ a series of
econometric analyses described below.
   5.3.1. Falsification Tests Based on Quarterly Financial Restatements. To assess
whether the associations we observe are driven by unobservable client
characteristics that differ between engagements with connected and un-
connected EQRs, we conduct several analyses. First, following Guo et al.
[2022], we examine whether the EQRs’ within-firm connections with
engagement partners affect the quality of unaudited client financial state-
ments. Because quarterly financial statements are subject to limited auditor
involvement, restatements of these statements can provide an indication of
pre-audit financial reporting quality (Pittman and Zhao [2021], Lisic et al.
[2022]).13 If the non-random assignment of connected EQRs to clients
with lower-quality financial reports drives the observed associations, we
would expect both quarterly and annual financial statements to exhibit
similar low quality.

shock that plausibly shifts the pool of eligible EQRs in that office without

   5.3.4. 5.3.4 Placebo Test. Finally, we conduct a placebo test to address
concerns about omitted variables that may coincide with the overall
EQR–client matching process. Following Cornaggia and Li [2019], we

7. Conclusion
   Engagement quality reviews are a critical component of an accounting
firm’s system of quality management. Using unique data on EQR identi-
ties and extensive signing records in China, this study examines whether
prior shared working experience between EQRs and engagement partners
has implications for audit quality and whether such experience factors into
EQR assignments. While such experience can facilitate efficient commu-
nication, increased familiarity may undermine effective monitoring if the
EQR is reluctant to challenge the engagement partners’ judgment or exer-
cise sufficient appropriate skepticism.
   Our results provide evidence of lower audit quality when the EQR has
prior shared working experience with the engagement partners. Mecha-
nism tests suggest these connections reduce scrutiny in audit planning and
execution, as evidenced by a higher likelihood of audit enforcement for
deficiencies in procedures, insufficient audit evidence, and lack of profes-
sional skepticism, as well as higher materiality thresholds. Additional anal-
yses show that these adverse effects primarily arise when previous shared
working relationships did not produce adverse outcomes, when EQRs are
not audit industry leaders, and when they face lower reputational risk. We
also find that prior shared working experience significantly increases the

     Audit Violation Classification and Case Identification Examples
   In China’s audit regulatory framework, supervisory authorities, such as
the China Securities Regulatory Commission (CSRC) and its regional of-
fices, routinely disclose detailed descriptions of audit violations and the
corresponding legal basis in their sanction announcements against indi-
vidual auditors or accounting firms. This regulatory transparency provides
a unique opportunity to further investigate the mechanism through which
EQRs influence audit quality. Specifically, this study leverages the detailed
information disclosed in sanction notices—such as the nature and basis
of violations—to explore how shared working relationships between EQRs
and the audit team shape audit outcomes.
   We utilize standardized violation classifications developed by the Easy-
Board database, which systematically codes the types of violations disclosed
in sanction notices. This approach allows for a more granular identifica-
tion of audit quality deficiencies. Table B1 in appendix B summarizes the
frequency distribution of audit violations based on a subsample of 681 ob-
servations. These observations correspond to audit engagements where ei-
ther the auditor or the client company was sanctioned in a subsequent year
due to issues with the financial statements of the focal year.
   The three most frequently cited violation types are deficiencies in audit
procedures (47.9%), insufficient audit evidence (37.4%), and lack of ap-
propriate professional skepticism (32.3%). These categories often involve
complex judgments or require heightened auditor skepticism, and thus are
particularly prone to review failure when EQR oversight is weakened—such
as in cases where close co-working relationships with the audit team impair
reviewer independence or effectiveness. By focusing on these violations,
we aim to better understand the mechanisms through which EQR–ENG
coworking relationships may compromise audit quality.
   To further illustrate the nature and documentation of audit violations,
appendix B also includes full-text English translations of two representative
enforcement cases issued by regional offices of the CSRC. These translated
